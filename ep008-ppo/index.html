PPO: reinforcement learning for a GPT

Our model learns from its own stories, scored as it writes them, with proximal policy optimisation, a critic and a penalty for drifting, and we measure it next to DPO.

Watch on YouTube

This time, our model learns from its own stories as it writes them, with reinforcement learning and proximal policy optimisation, PPO, as in the PPO paper and InstructGPT. A reward scored by code counts the requested words, a critic learns what score to expect, and a KL penalty keeps the model near the fine-tuned one. We train for ten minutes with a small penalty and with a large one, and measure both against the fine-tuned model and DPO.

Where PPO comes from

2:32 PPO comes from Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms, by John Schulman and others at OpenAI, from 2017. Its abstract describes policy gradient methods, which take turns between sampling data, such as our stories, and optimising an objective with gradient ascent. The standard methods make one gradient update per sample, and PPO's objective allows several passes of updates, in minibatches, over each batch of samples. It keeps some of the benefits of an earlier method, trust region policy optimisation, while being much simpler to implement, and the paper tests it on simulated robots and Atari games.

3:09 PPO is what trained InstructGPT, in Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback, by Long Ouyang and others at OpenAI, from 2022, which is the recipe behind the first ChatGPT. Its figure two shows the three steps of their method. First, supervised fine-tuning, on answers written by people, which is what we did in episode three. Then a reward model, trained on people's rankings of the model's answers, as people can't score each answer while the model trains. And last, PPO, which optimises the model against that reward. Our reward is going to be code that counts the words asked for, in place of a reward model. Equation two is the objective: the reward, minus beta times the log of how much more likely the new model makes the answer than the fine-tuned one, which is a penalty for drifting from it. There's also a term with the pretraining data, which we leave out.

4:04 In reinforcement learning, the model is called the policy, as it decides what to do, here which token to write next. The goal is to maximise the expected reward, the average score of the stories it would write for an instruction. The reward comes at the end of a story, from code that counts words, so we can't take its gradient the way we take the loss's. The policy gradient goes around that, by making each token of a story more likely in proportion to how well the story turned out. It follows the gradient of the token's log-probability, like fine-tuning does, weighted by A. A is the advantage: the reward the story got, minus V, the value, which is the reward a critic expected at that point in the story. A story that did better than expected has a positive advantage, and its tokens become more likely, and one that did worse, less likely. Subtracting what the critic expected leaves the gradient pointing where it did, on average, but makes it a lot less noisy, as a story that scores the way the critic expected teaches us very little.

PPO's clipped objective

5:05 PPO makes several updates from each batch of stories, so the model it updates drifts from the one that wrote them. Rho is the ratio of a token's probability under the model now to its probability when the story was written, and it starts at one. The objective multiplies the advantage by that ratio, so raising a good token's probability raises the objective. Clip keeps the ratio between one minus epsilon and one plus epsilon, with epsilon at zero point two, and the min takes the smaller of the two. So once a good token is twenty percent more likely than it was, pushing it further doesn't raise the objective any more, and for a bad token, pushing its probability down stops raising the objective once it's twenty percent less likely. The name comes from there: each update stays proximal, or close, to the model that wrote the stories.

The KL penalty

5:54 Last time, DPO's stories got longer and repeated the words, and beta was the single weight holding the policy near the reference. PPO keeps a frozen copy of the fine-tuned model too, as the reference, and r is the log of how much more likely the reference makes a token than the policy does. E to the r, minus r, minus one, estimates the K L divergence, which measures how far the policy's distribution has moved from the reference's. It's zero where the two agree, and above zero wherever they don't, as e to the r is at least one plus r. The loss has three parts: minus PPO's objective, the critic's squared error, weighted by a half, so that the critic learns to predict the rewards, and beta times the KL. With a small beta, raising the reward outweighs the penalty, and with a large one, the model stays close to where it started, so we're going to try two, zero point zero five and zero point five.
