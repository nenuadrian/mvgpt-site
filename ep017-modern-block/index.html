    RMSNorm

    3:50 RMSNorm comes from Root Mean Square Layer Normalization, by Biao Zhang and Rico Sennrich at the University of Edinburgh, from 2019. Their abstract says that LayerNorm helps a network train, but that it costs time, and their hypothesis is that one of its two steps, the re-centring, isn't needed. RMSNorm keeps the other step, re-scaling by the root mean square. In their experiments, it did about as well as LayerNorm, and took seven to sixty-four percent less time, depending on the model. Equation four is all of it: each of the inputs is divided by the root mean square of all of them, and multiplied by its gain, g, which the model learns.

    4:34 A layer norm works on each token on its own, on its three hundred and eighty-four channels, which the formula calls n. It takes their mean, mu, and their variance, sigma squared, which is the mean of their squared distances from mu. It subtracts the mean and divides by the square root of the variance, the standard deviation, so the channels come out with a mean of zero and a variance of one. Epsilon is a small number that keeps the division safe when the channels are all about equal. Then each channel is multiplied by its gain, g, and gets its bias, b, added, two numbers that the model learns for each channel. RMSNorm skips both the mean and the bias, and divides each channel by the root mean square of the channels, the square root of the mean of their squares. Then each channel is multiplied by its gain, which starts at one. So, before the gain, the channels come out with a root mean square of one, and their mean isn't moved to zero. When the channels' mean is zero, their variance is the mean of their squares, so the two norms divide by equal amounts. And RMSNorm computes one statistic instead of two, with half the parameters.

    QK-norm

    15:58 Normalising the queries and keys goes back to Query-Key Normalization for Transformers, by Alex Henry and others, from 2020, for translating languages with little training data. Their abstract says that the technique makes the softmax in attention less prone to saturating, which is when nearly all of a query's weight goes to a single key. They normalise each query and each key along the head's channels before multiplying them. Then they scale the result up by a parameter the model learns, instead of dividing by the square root of the dimension. Section three shows the problem, with an example of the softmax, which responds to the differences between its inputs. So seven hundred and sixty, seven hundred and fifty-two and seven hundred and fifty give the weights that twelve, four and two give, about ninety-nine point nine six percent on the first. A dot product has no upper bound, so as the scores grow, a difference that's small next to them can put nearly all the weight on a single key. Equation five is their attention, with the normalised queries times the normalised keys. Each score is then the cosine of the angle between a query and a key, between minus one and one, times g, the learned scale.

    17:06 The version recent models cite comes from Scaling Vision Transformers to Twenty-Two Billion Parameters, by Mostafa Dehghani and others at Google Research, from 2023. As they scaled up their vision transformer, they saw the training loss diverge after a few thousand steps, in models of around eight billion parameters. They traced it to extremely large attention logits, the scores that go into attention's softmax. The logits made the attention weights almost one-hot, with nearly all of a query's weight on a single key. To fix it, they applied a layer norm to the queries and the keys before the dot product, and kept the division by the square root of d. Figure one shows an eight-billion-parameter model with and without it. Without the norm, in red, the loss turns back up after about two thousand steps, and the largest attention weight goes to one. With it, in green, the loss keeps going down. Their appendix says that without the norm, the logits grew past fifty thousand. They also removed the biases of the query, key and value projections, and ran their layer norms without a bias or centring, citing Zhang and Sennrich. Dropping those made better use of their accelerators, by three percent, with no loss in quality. They kept the biases in the MLP, though, as they found them better for quality, at no cost in speed.

    18:24 A model doesn't have to be that large to run into it. Small-scale proxies for large-scale Transformer training instabilities, by Mitchell Wortsman and others at Google DeepMind, from 2023, studies two instabilities seen in large models. The growth of the attention logits is one of the two. Their abstract says that both show up in small models too, when they're trained with high learning rates, and that the fixes used at scale work there as well. Figure two shows a model of nine point four million parameters, smaller than ours, trained with a learning rate of zero point one. We pretrain with six times ten to the minus four. Without qk-layernorm, in orange, the largest attention logit climbs to about a million, and the loss jumps up. With it, in blue, the logit stays flat, and the loss stays down. Their hypothesis is that the logits grow with the square of the size of the weights, as the queries and the keys both grow with them, and a logit multiplies the two.

    19:21 In attention, a query's score against a key is their dot product, q, k transposed, divided by the square root of d k, the sixty-four channels of a head. That score is what these papers call an attention logit, and it has no upper bound. If training makes the query and key projections larger, the query and the key both grow, so the score grows with the square of their size. QK-norm runs each head's query and each head's key through RMSNorms of their own, one for the queries and one for the keys, over the head's sixty-four channels, before the dot product. Dehghani and others used a layer norm, and we use RMSNorm, like OLMo 2, from the Allen Institute for AI. Like them, we keep the division by the square root of d k. With its gains at one, an RMSNorm gives a head's query a root mean square of one. So the query's length is the square root of sixty-four, which is eight, and the key's is eight too. A dot product is at most the product of the two lengths, so the score stays between minus eight and eight, however large the projections' weights get.
