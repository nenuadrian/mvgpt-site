    RoPE: rotary position embeddings from scratch

    We replace our model's table of positions with rotary position embeddings, which turn each query and key by its position, so attention scores depend on how far apart two tokens are, and the KV cache can slide past the context window.

    Watch on YouTube

    Our model learned a table of positions that stops at its context window of 256 tokens, so today we replace it with rotary position embeddings, from the RoFormer paper. Each query and key is turned by an angle that grows with its position, so attention depends on how far apart two tokens are. We pretrain for ten minutes next to episode two's model, and measure both past their context window, where our KV cache can now slide.

    Rotary position embeddings

    3:17 Rotary position embeddings come from RoFormer, Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding, by Jianlin Su and others at Zhuiyi Technology, in Shenzhen, from 2021. Its abstract says that RoPE encodes each token's absolute position with a rotation, and that attention then depends on the relative position of two tokens, how far apart they are. Figure one shows how: the query and the key are split into pairs of channels, and each pair is turned by an angle that grows with the position, faster for some pairs and slower for others. And equation sixteen shows why it works: the query rotated for position m, times the key rotated for position n, comes out as the original query and key with a single rotation between them, by n minus m. Llama uses it, and so do most of the open models since.

    4:05 Take two of a query's channels as the two coordinates of a point on a plane, an arrow from the origin. Rotary embeddings turn that arrow by an angle, m times theta, where m is the token's position. So the token at position zero isn't turned, the next one is turned by theta, the one after by two theta, and so on. The arrow keeps its length, and its direction now carries the position. A head's sixty-four channels make thirty-two pairs, and each pair gets a theta of its own, ten thousand to the minus two i over sixty-four. The first pair turns by one radian per token, which takes it once around in about six tokens, and the last pair takes about forty-seven thousand tokens to go once around. Between them, the pairs tell near and far positions apart. Keys are turned like the queries, by their own positions. The score of a query with a key is their dot product, which for two arrows is their lengths times the cosine of the angle between them. Turn the query by m theta and the key by n theta, and the angle between them changes by n minus m, times theta, wherever the two tokens are. So a score depends on what the two tokens are and how far apart they are, and not on where they sit in the window. And a cache can slide because of it, as a key rotated for its position gives the correct score against each later query, however far the window has moved.
